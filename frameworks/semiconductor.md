# Semiconductor Framework

## 1. Industry Essence

Semiconductors convert design capability, manufacturing capacity, and supply-chain control into shipped units and pricing power. The buy-side question is which node in the chain captures incremental demand, which node faces bottlenecks, and whether pricing, mix, or volume can drive earnings revisions.

## 2. Core Growth Drivers

- End-market demand: AI, cloud, smartphones, autos, industrial, consumer electronics.
- Technology transition: process node, architecture, memory bandwidth, packaging, interconnect.
- Capacity cycle: wafer starts, utilization, tool availability, yield.
- Product mix: high-end chips, HBM, advanced packaging, custom ASICs, optical modules.
- Pricing cycle: ASP, contract pricing, spot pricing, supply-demand balance.
- Inventory cycle: customer restocking, digestion, channel inventory.

## 3. Business Model

Major models:

- Fabless design: monetize chip shipment volume and ASP, outsource manufacturing.
- Foundry: wafer volume x wafer price, with utilization and node mix driving margin.
- IDM: integrated design and manufacturing with product and manufacturing risk.
- Memory: commodity-like volume and price cycles, with mix and cost curve differentiation.
- Equipment/materials: capex cycle exposure, order backlog, installed base service revenue.
- Components/modules: unit shipment x ASP, often tied to product cycles and customer qualification.

## 4. Core Revenue/Profit Formula

Core formulas:

- Chip revenue = shipment volume x ASP
- Foundry revenue = wafer starts x wafer price x utilization
- Memory revenue = bit shipment x ASP per bit
- Equipment revenue = tool shipments + service revenue
- Module revenue = units shipped x ASP
- Gross profit = revenue x gross margin, driven by utilization, yield, mix, and pricing

Key sensitivities:

- ASP change.
- Shipment volume.
- Utilization rate.
- Yield and cost per wafer.
- Customer concentration.
- Backlog conversion.

## 5. Key Operating Metrics

- Shipment units, bit growth, wafer starts, utilization, yield.
- ASP, gross margin, operating margin.
- Book-to-bill, orders, backlog, lead time.
- Capex, capacity expansion, equipment delivery timing.
- Inventory days, channel inventory, customer inventory.
- Node mix, product mix, qualification status.
- Customer concentration and design wins.

## 6. Value Chain / Supply Chain

Value chain:

1. EDA and IP.
2. Design / fabless.
3. Foundry / wafer manufacturing.
4. Semiconductor equipment and materials.
5. Memory.
6. Advanced packaging and testing.
7. Components and modules such as optical modules, power semiconductors, sensors.
8. OEM / cloud / auto / industrial customers.

Buy-side questions:

- Is the bottleneck in design, wafers, packaging, memory, networking, equipment, or customer demand?
- Which listed company has the most direct exposure to the constrained node?
- Is demand incremental or just inventory restocking?

## 7. Listed Company Mapping

Map by value-chain role:

| Role | Positive exposure | Negative exposure | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fabless / chip design | Design wins, ASP, volume growth | Customer concentration, competition | Product revenue, customer commentary, shipment data |
| Foundry | Utilization, node mix, wafer price | Overcapacity, yield risk | Capex, utilization, node revenue mix |
| Memory | HBM / high-value mix, price recovery | Commodity price decline | Bit growth, ASP, inventory, contract price |
| Equipment | Capex orders, backlog | Capex cuts, export controls | Orders, book-to-bill, backlog |
| Packaging / modules | Bottleneck pricing, customer qualification | ASP erosion, capacity expansion | Capacity, qualification, shipment ramp |

## 8. Financial Impact Pathway

Signal -> demand or bottleneck -> shipment / ASP / utilization -> gross margin -> earnings revision.

Examples:

- AI accelerator demand -> HBM / packaging bottleneck -> higher ASP and backlog -> margin uplift.
- Optical module demand -> data center deployment -> unit shipment x ASP -> revenue mix shift.
- Foundry utilization recovery -> fixed cost absorption -> gross margin expansion.
- Inventory correction -> shipment decline -> margin compression.

Do not stop at "demand is strong"; specify which semiconductor node monetizes the demand.

## 9. Consensus vs Variant View

Consensus often tracks headline demand and obvious leaders. Variant view may come from:

- Underestimated bottleneck supplier.
- Underestimated ASP durability due to qualification barriers.
- Overestimated cycle durability when demand is inventory-driven.
- Misread margin impact because utilization or yield is ignored.
- Underappreciated negative exposure from capacity additions.

Ask:

- Is the market pricing volume, ASP, or both?
- Is earnings sensitivity higher in the bottleneck node than in the visible end product?
- What data would show the cycle has peaked?

## 10. Risks and Invalidation Triggers

- Customer inventory correction.
- ASP decline or contract price reset.
- Capacity expansion removes bottleneck pricing.
- Yield issues delay ramps.
- Export controls or geopolitical restrictions.
- Customer design loss.
- Capex cuts reducing equipment orders.
- End-market demand weakens.

## 11. Buy-Side Investment Judgment Framework

- Long candidate: clear bottleneck exposure, verified demand, pricing or margin upside, and consensus underestimates earnings leverage.
- Watchlist: strong technology or order signal but missing shipment, ASP, or margin validation.
- Too early / Need more evidence: signal is technical but not yet linked to revenue.
- Avoid: demand is known and fully priced, or exposure is indirect and weak.
- Short candidate: consensus expects durable growth but evidence points to ASP decline, inventory correction, utilization drop, or capex cuts.
